Flapping Airplanes and the promise of research-driven AI

Daniel Kim · January 25, 2026 · 5 min read

A new AI lab called Flapping Airplanes launched yesterday, and a Sequoia partner has an interesting take on why they stand out in a crowded field of AI startups. The lab's distinctive philosophy focuses on fundamental research rather than immediate productization—a contrarian bet in an era obsessed with shipping.

Flapping Airplanes emerges at a strange moment for AI research. The largest tech companies are spending billions on AI development, hiring researchers at unprecedented rates, and publishing papers at a furious pace. Yet some observers worry that the focus on practical applications is crowding out the fundamental breakthroughs that got us here.

Enter Flapping Airplanes, a new research lab founded by former OpenAI and Google Brain researchers with $100 million in seed funding from Sequoia Capital and other investors. The lab's mission is deliberately unfocused: pursue fundamental AI research without pressure to build products or generate revenue. It's a model that echoes earlier eras of research labs like Bell Labs or Xerox PARC—places that invented the future without worrying about commercializing it.

The Name

The lab's unusual name comes from a metaphor about early aviation. For centuries, would-be aviators tried to build flying machines by mimicking birds—crafts with flapping wings that never achieved sustained flight. The eventual breakthrough came not from better imitation of birds but from understanding the underlying principles of aerodynamics and building aircraft that exploited those principles, even if they looked nothing like birds.

The founders believe AI research is at a similar inflection point. Current AI systems work remarkably well, but we don't fully understand why. We're essentially building ever more elaborate machines that flap their wings harder, without understanding the aerodynamics of intelligence. Flapping Airplanes aims to discover those fundamental principles.

"Everyone else is building bigger language models. We're trying to understand why language models work at all. That's a fundamentally different endeavor."

The Research Agenda

Flapping Airplanes' research agenda spans several areas that the founders believe are underexplored. One focus is interpretability—developing tools to understand what's happening inside AI systems. Current models are essentially black boxes: we know they work, but we don't know how or why. The lab aims to make AI systems as transparent and understandable as classical algorithms.

Another priority is reasoning. Current AI systems are impressive pattern matchers, but they struggle with genuine reasoning—the ability to work through novel problems step by step. The lab is researching architectures specifically designed for reasoning rather than pattern matching, potentially drawing on symbolic AI approaches that have fallen out of favor.

The lab is also investigating AI safety from first principles. Rather than treating safety as an add-on to existing systems, they're researching architectures where safety is built in from the start. This includes work on aligned objectives, transparent decision-making, and systems that can explain their reasoning in human-understandable terms.

The Sequoia Perspective

What made Sequoia invest in a lab with no product plans and no clear path to revenue? Partner Stephanie Zhan says it comes down to a contrarian bet about where AI value will accrue. "Everyone assumes value will go to companies with the most data, the most compute, and the biggest models. We think there's an alternative: value goes to whoever makes fundamental breakthroughs that the giants are too pragmatic to pursue."

Zhan points to historical precedents. Google's search dominance didn't come from having more data or compute than competitors—it came from PageRank, a fundamentally better algorithm. DeepMind's AlphaGo wasn't about bigger neural networks but a novel architecture combining deep learning with tree search. Fundamental insights can trump brute force.

The Sequoia thesis is that AI is currently in a brute force phase—scaling up known approaches rather than discovering new ones. This phase will eventually hit diminishing returns. When it does, the labs that pursued fundamental research will be positioned to deliver the next wave of breakthroughs.

The Competitive Landscape

Flapping Airplanes isn't the only research lab pursuing this model. OpenAI was originally founded as a non-profit research lab before shifting toward product development. Anthropic maintains a strong research focus while also commercializing its technology. DeepMind continues fundamental research even as it develops products for Google.

What makes Flapping Airplanes different is the absence of any commercial pressure. The company has no products, no customers, and no revenue targets. Its only stakeholders are investors who explicitly signed up for long-term research without near-term commercialization expectations. This freedom to pursue curiosity-driven research is increasingly rare.

The lab also benefits from timing. Five years ago, fundamental AI research was primarily the domain of big tech companies. Today, AI capabilities have advanced enough that small teams can do meaningful research without massive infrastructure. Flapping Airplanes can rent compute rather than build data centers, focusing research budget on talent rather than hardware.

The Team

The founding team reflects the lab's research priorities. CEO Dr. Sarah Chen previously led interpretability research at OpenAI, publishing landmark papers on understanding neural network behavior. CTO Dr. James Okonkwo spent seven years at Google Brain working on reasoning architectures and neurosymbolic AI. The research team includes mathematicians, physicists, and cognitive scientists alongside computer scientists.

This interdisciplinary approach is deliberate. The founders believe AI breakthroughs will come from crossing traditional boundaries, combining insights from neuroscience, cognitive psychology, mathematics, and physics with computer science. The lab's hiring priorities reflect this: they're as likely to hire a theoretical physicist as a machine learning engineer.

The Skeptics

Not everyone is convinced that the pure research model can work in today's AI landscape. Critics point to the massive compute requirements of cutting-edge AI research—can a small lab compete with trillion-dollar companies? There's also the question of talent: can a startup attract top researchers when big tech offers significantly higher compensation?

Others question whether fundamental breakthroughs are actually needed. Maybe current AI approaches are sufficient for all practical purposes, and progress will come from incremental improvements rather than paradigm shifts. If this view is correct, labs like Flapping Airplanes are solving the wrong problem.

There's also the precedent of research labs that did brilliant work but failed to capture value. Xerox PARC invented the personal computer but Xerox didn't commercialize it. Bell Labs made fundamental discoveries in computing but AT&T reaped few benefits. Being right scientifically doesn't guarantee being successful commercially.

The Timeline

Flapping Airplanes has raised enough funding for roughly five years of operations at their current scale. The founders emphasize that this is a research timeline, not a product timeline. They don't expect to ship anything commercial in that period—research papers and fundamental insights are the goals.

This long horizon appeals to investors who worry that AI research is becoming too short-term oriented. "We need more labs thinking in decades, not quarters," says Zhan. "The most important AI breakthroughs often look useless at first. You need the freedom to pursue ideas that might seem crazy but could change everything."

What Comes Next

The lab's immediate priorities are hiring and establishing research infrastructure. They're recruiting researchers across technical disciplines, with particular emphasis on those willing to work on high-risk, high-reward projects. They're also building compute infrastructure and establishing collaborations with universities and other research institutions.

The first papers should appear within the next year, likely focusing on interpretability and reasoning. The founders emphasize that they're not trying to beat existing systems at benchmarks—rather, they're trying to understand why those systems work and how to build something fundamentally different.

Whether Flapping Airplanes will actually discover the aerodynamics of intelligence remains to be seen. But their launch reflects growing recognition that AI needs more than just bigger models and more data—it needs fundamental understanding. In an era obsessed with shipping, perhaps the boldest bet of all is taking the time to understand what we're building.
