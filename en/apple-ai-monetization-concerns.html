Guys, I don't think Tim Cook knows how to monetize AI

Sarah Chen · January 30, 2026 · 5 min read

One intrepid Morgan Stanley analyst dared to ask how Apple will make money on its AI investments. The answer will not shock you.

At Apple's latest earnings call, something unusual happened. A Morgan Stanley analyst asked the question that's been on everyone's mind: How exactly does Apple plan to make money from its massive investments in artificial intelligence?

Tim Cook's response was characteristically Apple—long on vision, short on specifics. The CEO spoke at length about how AI will "transform the user experience" and "reinvent how people interact with technology." But when pressed on the business model, the answers grew notably vaguer.

The Revenue Question

Apple has reportedly spent billions developing its AI capabilities, from the underlying machine learning infrastructure to the flashy consumer features showcased at WWDC. The company has acquired multiple AI startups, hired thousands of engineers, and built out significant compute infrastructure. Yet unlike competitors who charge subscription fees for AI features, Apple has remained silent on monetization.

"It's unclear to us how Apple generates meaningful revenue from AI in the near term," the Morgan Stanley analyst wrote in a follow-up note. "The company appears to be treating AI as a feature rather than a business, which may not satisfy investors looking for growth drivers."

The contrast with competitors is stark. OpenAI charges $20 per month for ChatGPT Plus. Microsoft bundles Copilot into its $30 per month Microsoft 365 subscriptions. Google offers AI features through its Google One subscription. Apple, meanwhile, has announced AI features for iPhone, Mac, and iPad—but has not mentioned any additional fees.

"If Apple is spending billions on AI but giving it away for free to drive hardware sales, that's a very different business model than what investors are expecting from the AI revolution."

The Hardware Play

Apple's traditional strategy has always been to use software and services to sell hardware. The company likely views AI as another pillar supporting iPhone and Mac upgrades rather than a standalone revenue stream. This strategy has worked brilliantly for decades—iOS features drive iPhone sales, which drive App Store revenue, which creates a virtuous cycle.

But AI may represent a different challenge. The most sophisticated AI features require substantial server-side processing and ongoing model training. These aren't one-time software updates—they're continuous investments in infrastructure and capabilities. If Apple isn't generating direct revenue from AI, those costs weigh on hardware margins.

Some analysts speculate that Apple will eventually introduce a premium AI tier, perhaps bundled with Apple One or iCloud+. The company already offers additional storage and features for $2.99 to $9.99 per month. An AI add-on could fit naturally into this tiered structure.

The Privacy Angle

Apple's biggest differentiator in AI could also complicate monetization. The company has emphasized privacy-first AI that processes as much as possible on-device rather than in the cloud. This approach reduces ongoing infrastructure costs but also limits the kinds of AI features Apple can offer compared to cloud-powered competitors.

On-device AI also makes it harder to upsell users to premium tiers—when the AI processing happens locally, there's less opportunity to gate features behind subscription paywalls. Apple could offer more powerful cloud-based AI features for a subscription, but that would require convincing users to trust Apple with their data in the cloud.

The Services Dilemma

Apple's services business has become a massive revenue generator, contributing tens of billions quarterly. But that business relies on clear value propositions: users pay for storage, entertainment, or exclusive content. AI features embedded in the operating system are harder to monetize directly.

One possibility is that Apple sees AI as essential infrastructure—the kind of capability that must be free to remain competitive. In this view, AI is like the App Store or Siri: foundational technology that enables other revenue streams rather than generating direct revenue itself.

But this strategy assumes that competitors won't gain an advantage by monetizing AI more aggressively. If users become accustomed to paying for AI capabilities from Google, Microsoft, or OpenAI, Apple's free offering may seem increasingly inadequate.

What Comes Next

Investors will likely continue pressing Apple for clarity on AI monetization. The company's silence on the topic is becoming increasingly conspicuous as competitors roll out increasingly sophisticated—and increasingly expensive—AI offerings.

Apple may simply be playing its usual game: wait for the market to mature, then enter with a polished product and clear business model. But the window for establishing a dominant position in AI is closing, and Apple's reluctance to discuss monetization suggests the company may still be figuring out its strategy.

The question isn't whether Apple's AI features will be impressive—they almost certainly will be. The question is whether impressive features are enough to justify billions in investment, or whether Apple will need to find a way to make AI pay for itself.
