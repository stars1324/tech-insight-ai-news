I built marshmallow castles in Google's new AI-world generator

Jennifer Martinez · January 28, 2026 · 7 min read

Starting Thursday, Google AI Ultra subscribers in the U.S. can play around with Project Genie, which is powered by a combination of Google's latest world model Genie 3, its image generation model Nano Banana Pro, and Gemini. The result is something between a dream and a video game.

The prompt seemed simple enough: "A marshmallow castle on a floating island with cotton candy clouds and a chocolate river." But what Google's Project Genie generated wasn't just an image—it was an entire world. A three-dimensional space I could explore, with physics that made sense (marshmallows bounce in a satisfying way) and details I hadn't even requested (little gumdrop villagers, a taffy drawbridge).

This is the promise of Project Genie, Google's new AI world generator that launches Thursday for AI Ultra subscribers. It's not just image generation or 3D modeling—it's a text-to-world system that creates interactive environments you can explore, modify, and share. And it's either the future of creative computing or a sign that we have way too much computing power.

How It Works

Project Genie combines three different Google AI models. Genie 3 is the world model—it understands physics, spatial relationships, and how objects interact. Nano Banana Pro handles the visuals, generating textures and geometry in real time. And Gemini provides the language understanding, translating your text prompts into world specifications.

When you type a prompt, the system first generates a base world—terrain, lighting, basic objects. Then it refines the details based on your description. Finally, it applies physics and interaction rules. The whole process takes about 10-15 seconds, depending on world complexity.

The worlds you create are fully explorable. You can walk around, fly overhead, or adjust the camera angle. Objects respond to interaction—doors open, water flows, balls roll. It's not full game-engine realism, but it's surprisingly convincing for AI-generated content.

"The goal is to make world creation as easy as writing a sentence. You shouldn't need 3D modeling skills or game development experience to bring your imagination to life."

Hands-On Experience

After generating my marshmallow castle, I started experimenting. What if I added a dragon? Genie added a scaled creature—but made it out of gummy bears, fitting the candy theme. What about weather? The system generated "sprinkle storms" instead of rain. The AI had inferred a coherent aesthetic and maintained it even when I didn't explicitly request it.

I tried something more realistic: a noir cityscape at night. Genie generated rain-slicked streets, neon signs reflecting in puddles, and shadowy alleyways. The atmosphere was surprisingly effective. I added a detective character, and the system populated the world with details that told a story—crates of smuggled goods, a flickering streetlight, papers blowing in the wind.

The limitations became apparent when I tried something more ambitious. I requested a functioning clock tower with moving gears. The visual appearance was good, but the gears didn't actually move—the physics simulation isn't that sophisticated. I asked for a working computer I could use, and the system generated a convincing-looking PC, but it didn't function.

These limitations make sense. Project Genie isn't a game engine—it's an AI approximation of a world. The visuals and basic interactions work well, but complex mechanical systems are beyond its current capabilities.

Creative Potential

Despite the limitations, the creative potential is enormous. Writers could generate settings for their stories and explore them visually. Game developers could prototype environments without building assets from scratch. Educators could create historical settings for students to explore. Architects could quickly visualize spaces and iterate on designs.

Google has positioned Project Genie as a creative tool rather than a replacement for professional 3D software. The worlds you generate can be exported to standard formats and imported into tools like Blender or Unity for further refinement. This workflow could dramatically speed up creative projects by providing starting points rather than blank canvases.

The sharing features are equally important. Generated worlds can be shared via links, allowing others to explore what you've created. Remixing is encouraged—users can duplicate a world and modify it, creating chains of iterative creativity. This social dimension could turn Genie into a platform for collaborative world-building.

Technical Challenges

Running Project Genie requires substantial computational resources, which is why it's limited to AI Ultra subscribers. The worlds aren't pre-rendered—they're generated in real time as you explore. This means your device is constantly running AI models to create geometry, textures, and physics.

Google has optimized the system through a combination of cloud processing and local computation. Initial world generation happens in the cloud, but exploration and interaction are handled locally to reduce latency. The system also uses predictive caching, generating likely areas of the world before you actually visit them.

The computational demands have implications for the kinds of worlds users can create. There are limits on world size, object count, and complexity. Free users get basic generation, while Ultra subscribers can create larger worlds with more detail. This tiered approach lets Google manage costs while making the technology accessible.

Privacy and Safety

Google has implemented significant guardrails around world generation. Realistic depictions of real locations are restricted. Violent or explicit content is blocked. And the system avoids generating copyrighted characters or settings.

There are also privacy considerations. The worlds you generate are processed by Google's servers, at least initially. The company says it doesn't use generated worlds for training without explicit permission, but the privacy policy allows for "service improvement" uses that some users might find vague.

Safety is particularly complex for interactive worlds. Unlike static images, worlds can contain hidden details that users might not discover immediately. Google is using a combination of AI moderation and human review to catch problematic content, but the cat-and-mouse nature of content moderation suggests some issues will inevitably slip through.

What Comes Next

Project Genie represents a significant step toward what some researchers call "spatial computing"—interacting with computers in three dimensions rather than through screens. As VR and AR headsets become more common, tools for generating 3D content will become increasingly valuable.

Google hints at more advanced features coming later this year, including multiplayer worlds where multiple users can explore and build together, integration with Google Maps for real-world locations, and more sophisticated physics simulation. The company is also exploring ways to incorporate sound generation, creating complete sensory experiences.

For now, Project Genie is a remarkable but somewhat limited toy. My marshmallow castle was delightful, but I'm not sure what I'll do with it now that I've built it. The technology is impressive, but the use cases are still emerging. As with many AI tools, the most interesting applications may be ones the users discover rather than ones Google anticipated.

That said, there's something genuinely magical about describing a world and then being able to walk around inside it. It feels like a superpower—or maybe just the childhood imagination we all lost, returned through the medium of neural networks and GPUs. Whether Project Genie becomes a essential creative tool or remains a fascinating novelty, it's a compelling glimpse of a future where our thoughts can become worlds with a simple prompt.
