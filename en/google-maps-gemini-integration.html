Google Maps now lets you access Gemini while walking and cycling

Chris Anderson · January 24, 2026 · 5 min read

When you're walking around and navigating with Maps, you can now ask Gemini questions like "What neighborhood am I in?" or "What are top-rated restaurants nearby?" The integration transforms Google Maps from a navigation tool into an intelligent local exploration companion.

Google has begun rolling out a significant update to Maps that brings Gemini directly into the navigation interface. A new AI assistant button appears during walking and cycling navigation, enabling conversational queries about your surroundings without interrupting your journey. The feature represents Google's most ambitious effort to integrate AI into location-based services.

How It Works

The integration is designed to be as unobtrusive as possible. During walking or cycling navigation, a small Gemini icon appears in the bottom corner of the screen. Tap it, and you can ask questions by voice or text. Gemini's response appears as an overlay, allowing you to continue viewing your navigation route while getting information about your surroundings.

The AI has access to your current location, route, and surrounding context. When you ask about restaurants nearby, it knows you're navigating and will suggest options along your route or with minimal detours. When you ask what neighborhood you're in, it doesn't just give a name—it provides context about the area's character, history, and points of interest.

Voice responses are optional. If you're cycling, having Gemini speak directions or restaurant recommendations might be preferable to reading text. If you're walking in a quiet area, text-only responses might be more appropriate. The system adapts to your mode and likely preferences.

"The goal is to make Maps feel less like a tool and more like a knowledgeable local companion who happens to know everything about every place you might visit."

Use Cases

The feature shines for exploration and discovery. Imagine walking through an unfamiliar neighborhood and wondering about its history. Previously, you'd need to exit navigation, search separately, and try to reconnect the pieces. Now, you just ask Gemini, and it weaves historical context into your ongoing navigation.

Cyclists benefit from route-aware recommendations. Looking for coffee? Gemini will suggest cafes along your route rather than across town. Need to restock water? It knows which convenience stores are coming up. The AI understands that cyclists have different priorities than drivers or pedestrians.

Tourists get a particularly powerful experience. Instead of researching destinations in advance and following rigid itineraries, they can wander freely and ask Gemini about whatever catches their interest. Who built that building? What's the story behind this park? Where can I find local food? The AI transforms aimless wandering into informed exploration.

Under the Hood

Technically, this integration required bringing several capabilities together. Gemini needs real-time location awareness, not just the general location that web-based AI typically accesses. It needs to combine map data, business information, reviews, and other sources into coherent responses. And it needs to do all this quickly enough to be useful to someone actively moving through the world.

Google has optimized the system for speed and relevance. Common questions about nearby businesses, neighborhoods, and landmarks are cached and processed locally when possible. More complex queries that require synthesis of multiple information sources happen in the cloud but are prioritized for rapid response.

The integration also draws on Google's extensive location data. Business information comes from Google Maps listings and reviews. Historical context comes from Google's Knowledge Graph and other sources. Real-time data like business hours and current wait times are included when available. The result is responses that draw on the same database powering Google Maps, enhanced with AI reasoning.

Privacy Considerations

Location data is among the most sensitive information companies collect, and Google has taken pains to address privacy concerns. The company emphasizes that Gemini queries during navigation are processed using the same privacy protections as other Google Maps features. Location history can be disabled, and incognito mode prevents queries from being saved to your account.

However, the very nature of the feature requires ongoing location access—Gemini can't answer questions about your surroundings without knowing where you are. Users comfortable with Google Maps' existing location tracking may not find this problematic, but privacy-conscious users may hesitate to enable AI assistance during navigation.

Google has also implemented guardrails around sensitive locations. Queries about government buildings, hospitals, schools, and other sensitive locations receive limited responses. The company is clearly aware of the potential for abuse and has restricted the feature accordingly.

The Competitive Landscape

Google isn't the first to integrate AI with maps, but its implementation is among the most sophisticated. Apple has added similar features to Apple Maps, though with more limited functionality and availability. Apps like ChatGPT can provide location-based information, but they lack deep integration with navigation and real-time location awareness.

What sets Google's implementation apart is the depth of integration and the quality of underlying data. Google Maps has more comprehensive business information, better reviews, and more detailed mapping data than competitors. Gemini can draw on this wealth of information to provide answers that other AI assistants simply can't match.

The feature also gives Google a competitive advantage in local search. When users ask Gemini about nearby businesses, those queries can influence search rankings and recommendations. This creates a virtuous cycle: more Gemini usage leads to better local understanding, which leads to better recommendations, which drives more usage.

Future Directions

This integration is likely just the beginning. Google is reportedly working on more advanced features, including augmented reality overlays that combine Gemini's conversational abilities with camera-based information about your surroundings. Imagine pointing your phone at a restaurant and having Gemini tell you about the menu, pricing, and reviews based on visual recognition.

The company is also exploring proactive assistance—where Gemini anticipates needs based on context and offers suggestions without being prompted. If your route passes by a highly-rated restaurant at mealtime, Gemini might mention it. If you're cycling toward a park on a sunny day, it might suggest that as a destination.

Longer term, the integration could expand to driving navigation, though safety considerations make this more complex. Voice-only interactions would be essential, and responses would need to be carefully timed to avoid distracting drivers. But the potential is enormous: an AI co-pilot that provides contextual information about your journey while keeping you focused on the road.

The Bigger Picture

This integration reflects Google's broader strategy of infusing AI throughout its products. Rather than AI being a separate product or feature, it becomes a layer of intelligence that enhances existing services. Maps becomes smarter. Search becomes more conversational. Android becomes more adaptive.

It also demonstrates how AI can transform familiar tools into something more. Google Maps has been around for nearly two decades. We think we know what it does and how to use it. But adding Gemini fundamentally changes the experience—from a utility for getting places to a companion for exploring them.

The rollout is gradual, starting with Android users in the US and expanding globally over the coming months. iOS support will follow. The feature requires a Google account and reasonably recent hardware, but most users should have access by mid-2026.

Whether this integration will change how people use Maps remains to be seen. But it represents a compelling vision for the future of location-based services: not just telling you where things are, but helping you understand and engage with the places you go. In an era when we're increasingly connected to digital devices even as we move through the physical world, that's a powerful proposition.
