Satya Nadella insists people are using Microsoft's Copilot AI a lot

David Park · January 29, 2026 · 6 min read

With Microsoft spending many billions on data centers, and rumors that no one is using its AI, CEO Satya Nadella shared some usage numbers that tell a more nuanced story.

Rumors have been swirling for months that Microsoft's Copilot AI assistant is seeing lackluster adoption. Enterprise IT managers quietly complain that users try it once or twice then abandon it. Analysts have questioned whether Microsoft's massive AI investments will ever generate returns that justify the expense.

At Microsoft's recent earnings call, CEO Satya Nadella finally addressed these concerns directly—not by denying them, but by sharing specific usage numbers that paint a more complicated picture than either the critics or the boosters would have you believe.

The Numbers

According to Nadella, Copilot now has "tens of millions of monthly active users" across Microsoft 365, Windows, and other products. The company says Copilot users are "significantly more productive" on average, with power users seeing "3-4x productivity gains" on specific tasks like document creation and data analysis.

Perhaps more importantly, Nadella revealed that Copilot usage is growing faster than any new Microsoft product in the company's history. The number of daily active users has doubled over the past six months, and the average user session length has increased by 40%.

"The engagement curves are exactly where we want them to be," Nadella told analysts. "Users who try Copilot tend to keep using it, and they use it more frequently over time. That's the indicator that matters most."

"We're not just seeing adoption—we're seeing sustained engagement. The people who use Copilot continue to use it, and they're finding more ways to incorporate it into their workflows."

The Reality Gap

So why the persistent perception that nobody's using Copilot? The answer lies in the gap between enterprise deployments and active usage, and in the difference between trying an AI feature and incorporating it into daily work.

Microsoft has sold Copilot licenses to tens of thousands of enterprises, many of which have thousands of employees. But enterprise software adoption is notoriously slow. Just because a company has purchased Copilot licenses for all employees doesn't mean those employees are actually using it—or using it effectively.

There's also the learning curve question. Copilot works best when users understand how to prompt it effectively, when to use it versus when not to, and how to incorporate its outputs into their workflows. Many users try Copilot a few times, don't get great results, and conclude that it's not useful. Microsoft is betting that as users become more AI-literate, adoption will accelerate.

The Use Case Question

Part of the challenge is that Copilot's value varies dramatically across different roles and use cases. For a software developer, Copilot's code suggestions can be transformative once properly configured. For an HR professional reviewing resumes, Copilot might provide modest time savings. For a sales representative, the value proposition may still be unclear.

Microsoft has been rolling out role-specific Copilot features designed to address this variability. Copilot for Sales integrates with CRM data and customer interactions. Copilot for Finance focuses on spreadsheet analysis and reporting. These targeted offerings aim to deliver clear value to specific professional personas rather than offering generic assistance to everyone.

The question is whether these specialized features will drive the kind of broad adoption Microsoft needs to justify its massive AI infrastructure investments. Usage may be growing, but it needs to grow substantially more to make the economics work.

The Infrastructure Bet

Microsoft has committed roughly $50 billion to AI infrastructure over the past two years, building out data centers packed with GPUs and investing in nuclear power to run them. These investments only make sense if AI usage reaches massive scale—and generates substantial revenue.

The company is betting that Copilot usage is currently in the early adopter phase and will accelerate as AI capabilities improve and users become more comfortable with AI assistance. This is similar to the early days of cloud computing, when skeptics questioned whether enterprises would ever trust their data to third-party infrastructure. Today, cloud is Microsoft's largest and most profitable business.

But the comparison to cloud has limits. Enterprises adopted cloud because it offered clear cost savings and operational benefits. Copilot's benefits are real but less quantifiable—productivity gains that are hard to measure and that vary widely across users and use cases.

Competitive Dynamics

Nadella's usage disclosure comes against a backdrop of intensifying AI competition. Google is integrating Gemini across its Workspace products. OpenAI continues releasing increasingly capable models. Startups are targeting specific use cases with specialized AI tools. Microsoft needs Copilot to succeed not just as a product, but as a platform that keeps users in the Microsoft ecosystem.

The company's strategy is to make AI ubiquitous across its products—to make Copilot the default way users interact with Windows, Office, Teams, and other Microsoft software. This ubiquity creates a moat: even if competitors offer better AI for specific tasks, users may stay with Microsoft because Copilot is already integrated into everything they use.

But this strategy depends on Copilot actually being good enough to retain users. If users find Copilot frustrating or limited, they may seek alternatives despite the integration advantage. Microsoft's usage numbers suggest things are moving in the right direction, but there's still a long way to go.

What Comes Next

Microsoft is reportedly working on major Copilot improvements for later this year, including better memory and context awareness, more sophisticated reasoning capabilities, and deeper integration with third-party applications. These upgrades could address many of the current limitations that hold back adoption.

The company is also investing heavily in AI education and change management for enterprise customers. Microsoft has deployed teams to help organizations design AI rollouts, train users, and measure productivity gains. This hands-on approach reflects recognition that technology alone isn't enough—successful adoption requires organizational change.

Nadella's message is clear: Microsoft's AI strategy is working, Copilot adoption is real and growing, and the company's massive infrastructure investments will pay off over time. The numbers support that story, but only partially. Usage is indeed growing, but it needs to grow much more to justify Microsoft's bets. The next year will be crucial in determining whether Copilot becomes the foundational technology Microsoft envisions or remains a niche tool for specific use cases.

Either way, Nadella's disclosure provides the most detailed look yet at how AI adoption is actually proceeding in the enterprise. The reality is neither the disaster critics claim nor the unqualified success boosters suggest. It's something in between: a new technology that's finding its footing, with adoption that's real but measured, and potential that's substantial but not guaranteed. That's probably exactly what you'd expect at this stage of a transformative technology's evolution.
