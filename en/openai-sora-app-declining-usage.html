OpenAI's Sora app is struggling after its stellar launch

Mark Thompson · January 28, 2026 · 6 min read

New data suggests the app is seeing declines in both app downloads and consumer spending, as the early hype about the AI video social network wears off and users question whether AI-generated content can sustain a social platform.

When OpenAI launched Sora last fall, the app seemed like an instant phenomenon. It shot to the top of app stores, generated millions of downloads in its first week, and was hailed as the future of social media—an AI-powered platform where anyone could create stunning videos with simple text prompts.

Three months later, the picture looks very different. Sora's download numbers have declined for six consecutive weeks. In-app purchases are down 65% from the peak. Daily active users have dropped roughly 40% since the launch high. The app that was supposed to revolutionize social video is struggling to maintain momentum.

The Hype Cycle

Sora's launch was masterfully orchestrated. OpenAI released a series of demo videos that looked impossibly good—cinematic quality output from simple text prompts. The company partnered with influencers who generated viral content. Tech press coverage was overwhelmingly positive, with some commentators declaring Sora the "TikTok killer."

The initial downloads reflected this hype. Millions of users rushed to try the technology, and social media flooded with Sora-generated videos. For a few weeks, Sora seemed like it might actually fulfill its promise as a new kind of creative platform.

But the novelty quickly wore off. Users discovered that generating good video required practice and skill. The app's free tier was limited—generating just a few short clips per day—and many users weren't willing to pay for premium tiers. And critically, watching AI-generated videos turned out to be less interesting than making them, creating a consumption problem for the platform.

"The fundamental challenge is that AI-generated content lacks the human element that makes social media engaging. We watch TikTok not just for the content but for the people behind it. Sora videos don't have that human connection."

The Technical Reality

Beneath the hype, Sora faced significant technical limitations. Video generation is computationally expensive, and OpenAI had to balance quality with speed and cost. The free tier generated low-resolution, short clips with visible artifacts. Premium tiers produced better output but at substantial cost—$19.99 per month for 100 generations or $49.99 for unlimited generations.

Users also discovered that prompting Sora effectively required practice. The difference between a mediocre video and a stunning one often came down to subtle adjustments in prompt wording, lighting specifications, and camera directions. Casual users unwilling to master prompt engineering found their results disappointing.

Generation times varied from seconds to minutes depending on queue length and output quality. The waiting experience, particularly during peak usage when delays could stretch to ten minutes or more, tested user patience. Many users simply abandoned the app rather than wait for generations to complete.

The Content Problem

Even more fundamentally, Sora struggled with the basic social media question: Why would anyone watch this? TikTok works because human creators develop distinctive styles, personalities, and narrative arcs over time. Sora videos are one-off generations with no continuity between them.

Some users tried to develop consistent characters or visual styles, but the AI's variability made this challenging. A prompt that generated a particular character one day might produce something substantially different the next. This inconsistency made it difficult to build followings or create series content.

The content moderation challenge also became apparent. While OpenAI implemented filters to prevent explicit or harmful content, creative users found ways to generate problematic material. The platform saw incidents of deepfakes, misleading political content, and copyrighted material. Each controversy drove away users concerned about the platform's safety and ethics.

The Competitive Landscape

Sora also faces mounting competition. Google has integrated similar video generation into YouTube and other products. Adobe has added AI video tools to Creative Cloud. Startups like Runway and Pika offer more specialized video generation tools, often with better pricing or features for professional users.

Perhaps more threatening, established social platforms are adding AI creation tools. TikTok has launched AI effects and filters that let users enhance their videos without leaving the platform. Instagram is testing AI-generated backgrounds and transitions. These integrated tools may satisfy casual users' curiosity without requiring them to download a separate app.

For professional users—video editors, marketers, content creators—dedicated tools with finer control and better integration with production workflows may be more appealing than a consumer-focused app like Sora. The platform risks being caught between two markets: not good enough for professionals, too complex for casual users.

The Monetization Question

OpenAI's business model for Sora remains unclear. The app generates revenue through subscriptions, but if users are churning quickly, subscription revenue becomes difficult to sustain. Advertising is an option, but AI-generated content creates brand safety challenges that advertisers may find unacceptable.

The computational costs of video generation are substantial. Each generation requires running massive AI models on expensive GPU infrastructure. If subscription revenue doesn't cover these costs, Sora becomes a money pit rather than a revenue source.

OpenAI might be using Sora as a loss leader to demonstrate AI capabilities and collect training data. From this perspective, the app doesn't need to be profitable—it just needs to generate useful data and showcase what OpenAI's technology can do. But this strategy only works if investors are patient with losses, and patience for unprofitable AI products may be wearing thin.

What Comes Next

OpenAI hasn't publicly addressed Sora's declining numbers, but the company is reportedly working on significant updates. These include improved generation quality, faster processing, and new features like character consistency and video editing capabilities. The company is also exploring social features like comments, likes, and follows to build community engagement.

Whether these updates will reverse the decline remains uncertain. The fundamental challenge—that AI-generated content lacks human connection—may be difficult to overcome through feature additions. Sora may need to pivot from a general social platform to something more specific: a tool for creators, a educational platform, or a specialized market.

The broader question is whether AI-generated content can sustain any social platform at scale. We may discover that social media fundamentally requires human creators, and that AI is better suited as a tool for human creativity than as a replacement for it. If that's true, Sora's struggles may be a symptom of a larger reality: the future of social media will still involve humans, with AI playing a supporting role rather than taking center stage.

For now, Sora continues operating, and OpenAI has given no indication that plans for the app have changed. But the declining numbers suggest that the initial vision of an AI-powered social network may need significant revision. The hype has faded, and the hard work of building a sustainable platform begins.
