Music publishers sue Anthropic for $3B over 'flagrant piracy' of 20,000 works

Rachel Green · January 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Originally, these music publishers had filed a lawsuit against Anthropic over its use of about 500 copyrighted works. Now they've dramatically expanded the case, alleging the AI company systematically trained on 20,000 songs without permission or compensation in what they call "one of the largest mass copyright infringements in history."

The lawsuit has ballooned from a dispute over 500 songs to an existential challenge for Anthropic's business model. The publishers are seeking $3 billion in damages—roughly $150,000 per work, the maximum statutory penalty for willful copyright infringement. The case could reshape how AI companies train their models and potentially establish precedents for copyright liability throughout the industry.

The Expanded Allegations

When the original lawsuit was filed last year, it focused on a relatively narrow set of lyrics that Anthropic's Claude model could reproduce. Publishers claimed their copyrighted lyrics appeared in Claude outputs without attribution or compensation. Anthropic defended itself as fair use, arguing that its use was transformative and that the model had learned song patterns rather than copying specific works.

The amended complaint tells a different story. Publishers allege they've obtained internal Anthropic documents showing systematic scraping of copyrighted song lyrics from multiple sources, including licensed lyrics sites and music streaming platforms. Rather than incidental inclusion, the lawsuit claims Anthropic deliberately built a training dataset focused on commercially successful songs.

Even more damaging, the complaint alleges that Anthropic's internal documentation discussed licensing strategies but ultimately decided to proceed without permissions, calculating that the benefits of training on copyrighted material outweighed litigation risks. If these allegations are true, Anthropic's fair use defense becomes substantially weaker.

"This wasn't accidental infringement. It wasn't fair use. It was a calculated business decision to use others' creative work without permission or payment, building a multi-billion dollar company on the back of uncompensated songwriters."

The Discovery Process

How did publishers uncover the alleged internal documents? Through discovery, the legal process where parties in lawsuits exchange evidence. The original lawsuit allowed publishers to subpoena Anthropic's training records, internal communications, and data collection practices. The expanded complaint suggests that discovery revealed far more extensive infringement than initially suspected.

Anthropic has fought to keep certain documents confidential, claiming trade secret protection. But courts have generally been unsympathetic to trade secret claims when the alleged secret is unlawful activity. The more Anthropic tries to suppress training details, the more suspicious courts tend to become.

The discovery process has also reportedly revealed communications between Anthropic and other AI companies about copyright strategy. Publishers are seeking to introduce these communications as evidence of industry-wide coordination to infringe copyrights, which could have implications far beyond Anthropic.

The Technical Defense

Anthropic's legal strategy has evolved as the case has progressed. Initially, the company argued fair use and challenged the standing of music publishers to sue on behalf of songwriters. Those arguments remain, but Anthropic has also introduced technical defenses about how its models actually work.

The company claims that while Claude was trained on lyrics, it doesn't store or reproduce copyrighted works. Rather, the model learns statistical patterns about how lyrics are constructed—rhyme schemes, thematic elements, common phrases—that it can apply to generate original content. From Anthropic's perspective, this is no different than a human learning songwriting by studying existing songs.

The publishers counter that Anthropic's own internal documents contradict this claim. They allege that Claude can and does reproduce substantial portions of copyrighted lyrics, particularly for well-known songs. The complaint includes examples where Claude reportedly generated near-exact reproductions of popular song lyrics when prompted appropriately.

The Broader AI Industry Implications

This lawsuit has become a bellwether for AI copyright issues. While other cases have addressed visual art, code, and text, the music industry has traditionally been among the most aggressive copyright enforcers. How this case resolves will likely influence strategies throughout the AI industry.

If publishers prevail, AI companies may face pressure to license training data or develop methods to train without copyrighted material. Some companies are already exploring "consent-based" training datasets and compensation schemes for creators. A loss for Anthropic would accelerate these efforts dramatically.

Conversely, if Anthropic successfully defends its fair use argument, it could embolden AI companies to continue training on copyrighted works without permission. That would likely lead to more lawsuits and eventually to Supreme Court consideration of how copyright law applies to AI training.

There's also the question of liability. Courts could rule that Anthropic's pre-training use was infringing but that the company's current operations are permissible if it stops using the copyrighted data. Alternatively, courts could find that models trained on infringing data are themselves infringing, potentially forcing companies to retrain their systems from scratch.

The Legislative Angle

Beyond the courts, this case is prompting legislative activity. Members of Congress have expressed interest in clarifying how copyright law applies to AI training. Some proposals would require AI companies to disclose their training sources, while others would create new licensing regimes for AI training data.

The music industry has been particularly active in lobbying on AI copyright issues. The Recording Industry Association of America has advocated for stronger protections and mandatory licensing. Whether legislation passes before this case concludes is an open question, but the lawsuit has certainly increased political attention to AI copyright issues.

What's at Stake

For Anthropic, the $3 billion at stake exceeds the company's total funding to date. A loss at that scale could be existential, particularly if courts also issue injunctions requiring Anthropic to retrain its models without copyrighted material. The company would likely face similar lawsuits from other copyright holders, multiplying the potential liability.

For the music industry, this case represents a chance to establish precedents before AI becomes even more entrenched. The publishers know that if AI companies can train without permission now, it will be much harder to change those practices later. This case is about establishing rules for the AI era, not just recovering damages for past infringement.

For the broader AI industry, the uncertainty itself is problematic. Companies don't know whether they can legally train on copyrighted material, whether they need to license training data, or what their exposure might be for past training. This uncertainty makes investors nervous and could slow AI development until the legal questions are resolved.

The Path Forward

The case is likely headed toward trial later this year, barring settlement. Anthropic has strong incentives to settle given the potential damages, but settlement would require both sides to agree on terms—and the publishers may believe they can extract more favorable terms through litigation.

Any settlement would likely include substantial payment, commitments about future training practices, and possibly some form of ongoing licensing arrangement. The question is whether Anthropic can afford a settlement on the scale publishers are likely to demand.

This case won't be the last word on AI and copyright, but it may be the most important. The music industry's willingness to aggressively pursue infringement, combined with the substantial damages at stake and the detailed discovery process, makes this lawsuit a potential landmark. However it resolves, the decision will shape the relationship between AI and copyright for years to come.
